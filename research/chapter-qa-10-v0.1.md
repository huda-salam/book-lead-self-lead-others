# Chapter 10 QA — v0.1

## QA result

**Overall: PASS WITH REVISIONS**

Chapter 10 establishes COMM.LISTENING and correctly turns listening into an active process rather than passive silence.

## 1. Conceptual QA — PASS

The chapter clearly distinguishes:

- listening from agreement;
- acknowledgment from concession;
- validation from justification;
- understanding from accommodation;
- listening from emotional absorption.

The core loop is:

**Attend → Clarify → Reflect → Check → Respond**

This should remain explicitly identified as a book framework, not a validated listening protocol.

## 2. Evidence QA — PASS

Kluger et al. (2024) is highly relevant for workplace listening and perceived listening.

The chapter should preserve the distinction between:
- relationship outcomes;
- performance outcomes.

The meta-analysis does not establish that every listening behavior directly causes better performance.

Elfenbein (2023) provides relevant contextual support for interpretation and emotion in workplace interaction.

Frazier et al. (2017) is useful background for psychological safety, but should not be used to claim that listening alone creates psychological safety.

## 3. Listening ≠ agreement — PASS

This is a key canonical boundary.

The chapter should retain:

> “Acknowledgment bukan concession.”

And:

> “Validasi bukan pembenaran.”

These are useful book distinctions and should not be presented as formal technical definitions.

## 4. Emotional contagion boundary — PASS WITH REVISIONS

The title and examples use “ikut terbakar” as a practical metaphor.

Avoid presenting emotional contagion as a clinical mechanism unless specifically supported.

The operational point is sufficient:
- notice emotion;
- do not automatically adopt the speaker's interpretation;
- keep enough regulation to continue understanding.

## 5. Listening and accountability — PASS

Strong organizational application.

The chapter correctly shows that listening to a subordinate does not require removing standards, and listening to a superior does not require automatic agreement.

This is an important connection to the book's leadership context.

## 6. Complaints and labels — PASS

The chapter avoids turning complaints into diagnoses such as “toxic” or “narcissistic”.

It appropriately redirects toward:
- what happened;
- frequency;
- impact;
- attempted responses.

## 7. Boundary handling — PASS

The section on continuing to listen while setting a boundary is strong.

It avoids the false choice between:
- accepting abusive behavior;
- refusing to hear the underlying problem.

## 9. Red-team

### Does listening become agreement?
No.

### Does validation become justification?
No.

### Does listening mean absorbing every complaint?
No.

### Does listening eliminate accountability?
No.

### Does the chapter encourage over-analysis?
Limited risk; the loop is intentionally lightweight.

### Does “notice what is not said” become mind-reading?
Potential risk. The text correctly directs the reader to ask rather than infer. Preserve this boundary.

## 10. Practice QA — PASS

Acknowledge–Clarify directly mirrors the mechanism.

The exercise is practical and should remain identified as a book learning tool.

## 11. Retrieval QA

Questions:
- “Bagaimana mendengarkan orang yang sedang marah?”

- “Apakah mendengarkan berarti setuju?”

- “Bagaimana memvalidasi tanpa membenarkan?”
- “Kapan mendengar saja tidak cukup?”

Cases:
- colleague venting;
- subordinate reporting workload;
- superior giving an unclear instruction;
- person speaking aggressively;
- feedback that challenges the leader.

## 12. Terminology QA

Monitor:

- validation vs justification;

## 13. Editorial QA — PASS WITH REVISIONS

The chapter is practical and closely aligned with the user's training target of not “ngompori” people who are venting.

The phrase “venting → understanding → choice” is a useful pedagogical synthesis and should remain marked as such.

Final pass should reduce unnecessary English where Indonesian is clearer.
