# Evidence Update v0.4 — Learning, Retrieval, and Practice

## Retrieval practice
- McDermott (2021), Annual Review of Psychology, reviews evidence that practicing retrieval after learning slows forgetting and supports later retrieval across materials and learner groups. citeturn0search0
- Yang et al. (2021), Psychological Bulletin, meta-analysis of 222 classroom studies and 48,478 students, found an overall medium effect for testing/quizzing on academic achievement, with effects moderated by conditions such as feedback, repetition, format, and timing. citeturn0search1
- Pan & Rickard (2018), Psychological Bulletin, meta-analysis of 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments, found testing can support transfer, with transfer varying by test format and task type. citeturn0search4

## Implication for this book
Retrieval should not be treated merely as a navigation/search feature. It can also be part of the learning design: the reader should sometimes be asked to retrieve a principle, classify a situation, choose a response, or generate an interpretation before seeing the book's treatment.

## Important boundary
Evidence for retrieval practice concerns learning and memory. It does not by itself establish that the book's behavioral drills will automatically produce workplace behavior change. The book must distinguish knowledge retention, transfer, and real-world behavior change.

## Design decision
Adopt three retrieval layers:
1. **Navigate** — find the canonical knowledge.
2. **Retrieve** — recall or reconstruct the principle before rereading.
3. **Apply** — use the principle in a new case and review the response.

Therefore, Chapter 44 should not merely contain exercises at the end. Each chapter should contain a lightweight retrieval prompt, while Chapter 44 provides spaced review, scenario rotation, and personal response protocol.
