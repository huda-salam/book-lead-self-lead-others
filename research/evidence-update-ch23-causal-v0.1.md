# Evidence Update — Chapter 23: Mencari Akar Tanpa Terjebak Root Cause

## Evidence focus

Chapter 23 addresses causal reasoning in organizational problems. It warns against treating a single “root cause” as automatically sufficient, while also avoiding the opposite error of refusing to identify actionable causes.

### 1. Causal explanation requires more than sequence

That B happened after A does not establish that A caused B. Causal reasoning requires attention to alternative explanations, temporal ordering, mechanisms, confounding, measurement, and counterfactual thinking.

### 2. Complex organizational outcomes can have multiple contributing causes

A missed target, delayed process, or quality failure may arise from interacting factors rather than one root cause. The book should therefore prefer:
- contributing factors;
- necessary conditions;
- sufficient conditions where defensible;
- mechanisms;
- and leverage points.

### 3. “Root cause analysis” can become premature certainty

Tools such as the 5 Whys can be useful prompts for investigation, but should not be presented as universally valid causal inference methods. Repeatedly asking “why” does not guarantee that the final answer is the true root cause.

### 4. Mechanism matters

A useful causal explanation should answer:
> “How would this factor produce the observed outcome?”

This helps distinguish plausible stories from causal explanations.

### 5. Counterfactual questions improve causal discipline

Useful questions include:
- If this factor were different, would the outcome likely change?
- What else changed at the same time?
- What evidence would distinguish competing explanations?
- Is the proposed cause upstream, downstream, or merely correlated with the outcome?

### 6. Actionable cause is not necessarily ultimate cause

Leaders often need the factor they can change, not a metaphysical “first cause.” The most useful intervention point may be downstream from the original origin of the problem.

## Editorial implications

- Do not equate correlation with causation.
- Do not claim a single root cause without sufficient evidence.
- Do not let causal complexity become an excuse for inaction.
- Separate cause, contributor, symptom, mechanism, and leverage point.
- Use causal language calibrated to evidence: “consistent with,” “likely contributor,” “we have evidence that,” etc.
- When stakes are high, use more rigorous causal analysis or specialist investigation.
- In safety, fraud, legal, or technical incidents, preserve formal investigation requirements.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Outcome → Timeline → Candidate causes → Alternatives → Mechanism → Evidence → Leverage point → Test → Update**

This is a book working model, not a validated root-cause taxonomy.
