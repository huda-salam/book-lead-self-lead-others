# Evidence Update — Chapter 14: Berbicara ke Atasan, Rekan, dan Bawahan

## Evidence focus

Bab ini membahas komunikasi yang sama-sama memiliki tujuan substantif tetapi perlu menyesuaikan **arah hubungan, kewenangan, ketergantungan, risiko, dan kebutuhan informasi**.

### 1. Hierarchy affects voice and speaking-up
Pfrombeck, Levin, Rucker & Galinsky (2022) reviewed 113 articles after screening 546 abstracts and developed a Hierarchy of Voice framework. Their synthesis treats hierarchy, power, and status as central to whether people speak up, how they appraise the risk, and how the recipient reacts. This supports treating communication with superiors as different in risk and dynamics from peer communication. citeturn0search0turn0search4

### 2. Voice is not one undifferentiated behavior
Chamberlin, Newton & LePine (2017) meta-analyzed promotive and prohibitive voice and found that antecedents and associations differ between forms. Constructive voice was associated with leader behavior, workplace climate, attitudes, emotions, and individual factors; the authors also caution against treating voice as a single undifferentiated construct. citeturn0search2turn0search3

### 3. Supervisor-subordinate communication can produce silence
Bisel, Messersmith & Kelley (2012) proposed that command structures create relational contexts in which subordinates may hesitate to disagree with supervisors, producing silence or equivocation. This is useful for the chapter's distinction between respect for authority and surrender of professional judgment. citeturn0search7

### 4. Leader power/status and employee voice
Rong, Sui & Jiang (2022) used two experiments and a multi-source, multi-wave field survey and reported different relationships for leader power and leader status with subordinate voice. This supports careful treatment of power and status as distinct variables rather than assuming that all hierarchy has the same communication effect. citeturn0search5

### 5. Upward communication is situational
Kumar (2017), using a multiphase study with 1,285 respondents, found that subordinate-superior communication behavior varied with perceived superior power, organizational politics, and political skill; the study also emphasized environmental conditions that make communication easier or harder. This supports the chapter's practical attention to context rather than personality-only explanations. citeturn0search9

### 6. Leader response matters
Research on speaking up to higher-ups found that leaders from immediate supervisors to skip-level managers can influence whether employees perceive voice as risky or futile. This supports treating the listener's behavior as part of the communication system rather than putting all responsibility on the speaker. citeturn0search10

## Editorial implications

- Do not frame communication style as fixed personality matching.
- Do not equate upward communication with obedience, flattery, or political maneuvering.
- Do not equate downward communication with command-only behavior.
- Do not treat hierarchy as proof that a superior is correct or a subordinate is less competent.
- Distinguish **content** from **delivery** and **authority** from **truth**.
- Adapt communication to objective, risk, authority, information asymmetry, and relationship without changing factual standards.
- Preserve the distinction between constructive voice, warning/risk voice, disagreement, request, update, and instruction.
- Avoid causal overclaiming from observational, qualitative, or cross-sectional evidence.
- Cultural and organizational context may change how hierarchy and directness are interpreted.

## Proposed book synthesis

A practical framework for the chapter may be:

**Audience → Objective → Authority → Risk → Information → Delivery → Confirmation**

This is a book working model, not a validated taxonomy.
