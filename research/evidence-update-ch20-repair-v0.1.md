# Evidence Update — Chapter 20: Memperbaiki Hubungan Setelah Kesalahan

## Evidence focus

Chapter 20 treats relational repair as a leadership capability after mistakes, conflict, broken expectations, or trust disruption. Repair is not defined as restoring the relationship to its previous state at any cost. The aim is to acknowledge what happened, correct what can be corrected, rebuild predictability, and learn.

### 1. Repair is more than saying sorry

Research on apologies and relationship repair suggests that apology can facilitate reconciliation, but its effects depend on context, perceived sincerity, responsibility, harm, and subsequent behavior. An apology without corrective action can be insufficient.

### 2. Trust can be repaired through behavior

Organizational trust research generally treats trust as related to expectations about another party's future behavior. After a violation, repair can therefore involve explanation, acknowledgment, corrective action, safeguards, and consistent subsequent behavior.

### 3. Not every rupture should be repaired in the same way

The appropriate response depends on:
- severity of harm;
- responsibility;
- intent versus impact;
- repetition;
- power asymmetry;
- willingness to repair;
- safety;
- and whether the relationship remains viable.

The book should avoid universal “forgive and reconcile” messaging.

### 4. Accountability and repair can coexist

Repair does not erase accountability. A leader can say:
> “Saya salah,”
while also saying:
> “Kita tetap perlu memperbaiki proses agar ini tidak terjadi lagi.”

Conversely, formal accountability does not automatically eliminate the need for relational repair where continued collaboration remains necessary.

### 5. Psychological safety and trust are related but distinct

Frazier et al. (2017) meta-analysis supports the importance of psychological safety, but Chapter 20 should not claim that apology or repair automatically restores psychological safety or trust. Those are outcomes that may require repeated evidence over time.

## Editorial implications

- Acknowledge the concrete event before explaining intent.
- Own one's contribution without claiming responsibility for what one did not do.
- Do not use “sorry” to close a conversation prematurely.
- Distinguish apology, explanation, correction, restitution, and prevention.
- Make corrective commitments observable.
- Do not demand immediate forgiveness.
- Do not force reconciliation where safety or formal accountability requires distance.
- Rebuild trust through predictable behavior, not persuasion alone.
- Repair may be partial; some consequences cannot be reversed.
- The injured party retains agency over whether and how the relationship continues.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Acknowledge → Own → Repair → Prevent → Rebuild → Review**

This is a book working model, not a validated repair protocol.
