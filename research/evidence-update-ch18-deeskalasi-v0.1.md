# Evidence Update — Chapter 18: Menurunkan Suhu Konflik

## Evidence focus

Chapter 18 focuses on de-escalation as a response capability after Chapter 17 has diagnosed the conflict. The goal is not to suppress disagreement, but to reduce unnecessary escalation so that substantive issues can be handled.

### 1. Constructive conflict management involves open-minded discussion

Tjosvold, Wong & Chen (2014) review conflict-management research and identify open-minded discussion—expressing one's own view, listening to opposing views, understanding them, and attempting integration—as one route associated with constructive conflict management. They also emphasize that conditions matter and that open-mindedness is not appropriate in every situation. citeturn0search3

### 2. Peacemaking is broader than calming emotions

Zhang et al. (2018) systematic review identifies four components of workplace peacemaking: relational, procedural, emotional, and content help. This supports a broader view of de-escalation: reducing tension may require clarifying process or substance, not merely asking people to calm down. citeturn0search1turn0search4

### 3. Psychological safety creates room for interpersonal risk-taking, but is not the same as comfort

Frazier et al. (2017) meta-analysis covered 136 independent samples, more than 22,000 individuals, and nearly 5,000 groups. Psychological safety is relevant to speaking and interpersonal risk, but Chapter 18 should not equate it with absence of challenge, pressure, or accountability. citeturn0search8

### 4. Emotion regulation is relevant but suppression is not a universal solution

Research on workplace emotion regulation indicates that how emotions are managed can affect interaction processes. Recent work on relationship conflict distinguishes response-focused suppression from antecedent-focused reappraisal, with context-dependent consequences. This supports the book's rule: regulate escalation without pretending the underlying issue does not exist. citeturn0search16

### 5. Serious workplace aggression requires more than conversational technique

A 2026 systematic/integrative review of workplace aggression and conflict identifies role, interpersonal, organizational, leadership, and individual antecedents and emphasizes organizational measures alongside individual coping. A 2021 systematic review of workplace-violence de-escalation interventions similarly found that training alone had limited impact and that multicomponent interventions were more useful. These findings are from specific contexts and should not be generalized to ordinary disagreements. citeturn0search2turn0search13

## Editorial implications

- De-escalation ≠ surrender.
- De-escalation ≠ avoiding disagreement.
- De-escalation ≠ making everyone feel comfortable.
- De-escalation is not a substitute for decision, boundary, investigation, or formal escalation.
- Lower the emotional temperature without lowering standards.
- Separate the person from the issue.
- Slow the interaction when speed is amplifying error, but do not create delay when immediate action is required.
- Acknowledge experience without automatically conceding the claim.
- Ask clarifying questions before rebutting.
- Reframe from accusation to observable issue.
- Use process, timeouts, written summaries, mediation, or third-party help when appropriate.
- When there is threat, violence, serious misconduct, or safety risk, prioritize safety and formal procedures over conversational technique.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Stabilize → Clarify → Narrow → Reframe → Choose next step**

This is a book working model, not a validated intervention protocol.
