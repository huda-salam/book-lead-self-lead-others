# Evidence Update — Chapter 21: Keluhan Bukan Selalu Masalah

## Evidence focus

Chapter 21 introduces problem framing. A complaint is information about an experience, observation, perceived gap, or desired change; it is not automatically a well-defined problem. The leader's task is to translate complaints into testable problem statements without dismissing the experience.

### 1. Problem framing changes what gets solved

Research in judgment and decision making shows that how a problem is represented can influence the options considered and the resulting decisions. Framing is therefore not cosmetic; it can alter the decision space.

### 2. Symptoms, problems, causes, and solutions are different

A complaint such as “the process is too slow” may describe a symptom. The underlying problem may involve approval bottlenecks, unclear requirements, capacity, sequencing, or a different performance objective.

The book should avoid assuming that the first explanation is the root cause.

### 3. Problem definition should preserve the user's experience without treating interpretation as fact

A useful leadership move is:

> “Saya dengar keluhannya. Sekarang mari kita pastikan apa kondisi yang sebenarnya ingin kita ubah.”

This connects Chapter 10 listening with Chapter 21 problem framing.

### 4. Not every complaint is a problem to solve

A complaint can be:
- an observation;
- an emotional expression;
- a request;
- a preference;
- a signal of unmet need;
- a legitimate risk;
- or an inaccurate interpretation.

Different types call for different responses.

### 5. Problem statements benefit from observable conditions and desired states

A practical problem statement should identify:
- current observable condition;
- affected party/process;
- desired condition;
- relevant constraint;
- and evidence available.

This is a book tool, not a universal validated formula.

## Editorial implications

- Do not dismiss complaints as whining.
- Do not treat every complaint as a complete problem definition.
- Separate symptom from problem and problem from cause.
- Avoid jumping directly to a solution.
- Use measurable/observable language where possible.
- Preserve uncertainty when the evidence is incomplete.
- A preference does not need to become a system problem.
- A recurring complaint may reveal a structural problem even when each individual complaint sounds minor.
- Some problems are actually decisions, trade-offs, policy disputes, or boundary issues.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Listen → Translate → Bound → Test → Frame**

This is a book working model, not a validated problem-framing taxonomy.
