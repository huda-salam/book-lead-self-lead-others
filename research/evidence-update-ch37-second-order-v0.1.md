# Evidence Update — Chapter 37: Melihat Dua Langkah ke Depan

## Evidence focus

Chapter 37 treats second-order thinking as a leadership and management capability: considering not only the immediate effect of an action, but plausible downstream consequences, interactions, feedback, dependencies, and reversals.

### Core evidence posture

- Decisions can generate indirect consequences beyond the immediate intended outcome.
- Interventions can alter incentives, behavior, workload, information flow, and dependencies.
- Short-term optimization can create longer-term costs.
- Unintended consequences are not always predictable; second-order thinking is therefore a discipline of anticipating plausible consequences, not claiming certainty.
- Feedback loops can amplify or dampen effects.
- Stakeholders may adapt to a new rule, incentive, or process.
- A decision can change the future decision environment itself.
- Scenario analysis and pre-mortem methods can help surface plausible failure modes, but they do not predict the future.
- Reversibility and monitoring can reduce the cost of uncertainty.

## Editorial implications

1. Ask “then what?” at least once beyond the immediate outcome.
2. Identify who adapts to the decision and how.
3. Look for incentive effects.
4. Check dependencies and bottlenecks.
5. Distinguish direct consequence from plausible downstream consequence.
6. Do not turn second-order thinking into endless speculation.
7. Use a small number of plausible scenarios.
8. Identify leading indicators and review triggers.
9. Prefer reversible experiments when uncertainty is high and stakes permit.
10. Second-order thinking complements, not replaces, execution.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Decision → First-order Effect → Adaptation → Second-order Effect → Interaction → Risk/Opportunity → Indicator → Review**

This is a book working model, not a validated second-order-thinking taxonomy.
