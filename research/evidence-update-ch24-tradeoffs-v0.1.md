# Evidence Update — Chapter 24: Memilih Solusi dan Mengakui Trade-off

## Evidence focus

Chapter 24 addresses choice after problem framing and causal analysis. It teaches that choosing a solution is not the same as finding an option with no downside. Decisions allocate scarce resources, accept some risks, and reject alternatives.

### 1. Decisions involve trade-offs

Decision analysis treats alternatives as bundles of consequences under constraints. A choice can improve one objective while worsening another. The relevant question is therefore not “which option is perfect?” but “which trade-off is acceptable given the objectives, constraints, uncertainty, and reversibility?”

### 2. Multi-criteria decisions require explicit criteria

When alternatives differ across cost, speed, quality, risk, control, equity, maintainability, or strategic fit, implicit weighting can hide the real disagreement. Making criteria explicit can improve transparency, while scores should not be presented as objective truth.

### 3. Uncertainty should be represented, not hidden

Expected outcomes are often uncertain. Decision quality should therefore consider ranges, scenarios, sensitivities, or confidence rather than only point estimates when the stakes justify it.

### 4. Constraints can be legitimate

Budget, law, safety, capacity, time, technical compatibility, and organizational authority can eliminate otherwise attractive options. Constraints should be distinguished from preferences.

### 5. Value depends on context and decision maker

The same option can be reasonable under one objective and unreasonable under another. The book should avoid universal “best solution” language.

### 6. Reversibility and option value matter

A reversible decision can preserve future choices. An irreversible decision may justify more analysis or stronger evidence. This connects Chapter 9 and Chapter 22 to solution choice.

### 7. Decision quality is not outcome quality

A well-reasoned decision can produce a poor outcome because of uncertainty or external events. Conversely, a lucky outcome does not prove the decision process was sound. Chapter 26 will own post-outcome review.

## Editorial implications

- State objectives before comparing options.
- Separate hard constraints from preferences.
- Make major trade-offs visible.
- Avoid false precision in scoring.
- Use qualitative comparison when quantitative estimates are weak.
- Consider downside, not only expected upside.
- Consider who bears the cost and who receives the benefit.
- Document why an option was selected and what was deliberately not optimized.
- Do not pretend that every disagreement is factual; some are legitimate value/priority differences.
- Preserve uncertainty and confidence.
- Match analysis depth to stakes and reversibility.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Objective → Constraints → Criteria → Alternatives → Trade-offs → Risk/Uncertainty → Choice → Rationale → Review trigger**

This is a book working model, not a validated decision taxonomy.
