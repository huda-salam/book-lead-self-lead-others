# Evidence Update — Chapter 28: Kepentingan, Kekuasaan, dan Pengaruh

## Evidence focus

Chapter 28 explains organizational power and influence as descriptive realities that leaders need to understand, while maintaining a clear ethical boundary between influence and manipulation.

### 1. Authority is only one source of power

Formal authority is one basis of influence. Organizational research also examines dependence, control of resources, expertise, information, relationships, access, and network position. These sources can coexist and vary by context.

### 2. Power is relational and contextual

Power is not simply a personality trait possessed in a fixed amount. It depends partly on what another actor needs, what resources or alternatives exist, and how relationships and organizational structures are configured.

### 3. Interests are not automatically unethical

Different actors can legitimately prioritize different outcomes because they have different roles, responsibilities, risks, constraints, or incentives. Interest conflict therefore should not automatically be interpreted as bad faith.

### 4. Influence is broader than coercion

Influence can occur through reasoning, evidence, framing, consultation, coalition-building, expertise, relationship, incentives, authority, and negotiation. The ethical question is not whether influence exists but how it is exercised and what constraints apply.

### 5. Dependence matters

If one actor controls something another actor needs, bargaining position can change. Alternatives and substitutability therefore matter when reading organizational power.

### 6. Information asymmetry matters

People can have different information because they occupy different roles. Information asymmetry can improve specialization but can also create influence advantages when information is selectively disclosed or controlled.

### 7. Ethical influence requires transparency and legitimate purpose

Influence becomes problematic when it relies on deception, coercion, retaliation, concealed conflicts of interest, abuse of authority, or deliberate exploitation of vulnerabilities.

## Editorial implications

- Teach power literacy without teaching political manipulation.
- Separate formal authority, dependence, expertise, information, network access, and relational influence.
- Treat interests as legitimate differences until evidence indicates misconduct or bad faith.
- Distinguish persuasion, negotiation, coalition-building, and coercion.
- Encourage mapping of stakeholders and dependencies before acting.
- Preserve governance, fairness, documentation, and accountability.
- Do not present “office politics” as a game where winning is the objective.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Actor → Interest → Resource → Dependence → Authority → Information → Network → Influence → Constraint**

This is a book working model, not a validated power taxonomy.
