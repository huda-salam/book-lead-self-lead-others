# Evidence Update — Chapter 31: Ownership dan Kredibilitas

## Evidence focus

Chapter 31 treats ownership and credibility as leadership capabilities expressed through behavior over time. It avoids equating ownership with taking blame for everything or credibility with confidence and image.

### 1. Ownership is broader than blame

Ownership can include acknowledging responsibility, clarifying what is and is not within one's control, taking appropriate action, communicating status, and following through. It should not become indiscriminate self-blame.

### 2. Credibility is relational and evidence-based

Leadership credibility can be shaped by perceived competence, integrity, reliability, consistency, transparency, and behavior over time. No single behavior proves credibility.

### 3. Reliability is built through repeated commitments

A leader's credibility is affected by whether stated commitments, expectations, and actions align. Repeated overpromising can damage trust even when intentions are good.

### 4. Transparency includes calibrated uncertainty

Credibility does not require pretending to know everything. A leader can say:
- what is known;
- what is uncertain;
- what is being checked;
- and when an update will follow.

This can be more credible than false certainty.

### 5. Ownership includes bad news

Leaders who surface problems early preserve decision capacity. Ownership should therefore include reporting material risks and deviations rather than hiding them until a solution is available.

### 6. Credit and accountability

Credible leadership includes giving appropriate credit to contributors and accepting appropriate responsibility for one's decisions. Ownership should not become credit-taking or blame-shifting.

### 7. Repair matters

When a leader is wrong, correction, explanation, repair, and changed behavior are part of credibility rebuilding. A single apology is not equivalent to restored trust.

### 8. Context matters

Ownership is bounded by actual authority, resources, role, and information. Leaders should not claim responsibility for outcomes they could not reasonably influence, while still taking responsibility for their own choices and escalation.

## Editorial implications

- Distinguish ownership from total blame.
- Distinguish credibility from charisma.
- Make commitments realistic and observable.
- Surface bad news early.
- Admit uncertainty without abandoning responsibility.
- Give credit accurately.
- Correct errors visibly and proportionately.
- Avoid performative ownership language without behavioral follow-through.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Commitment → Clarity → Follow-through → Transparency → Accountability → Repair → Consistency**

This is a book working model, not a validated credibility taxonomy.
