# Evidence Update — Chapter 19: Batas: Tegas Tanpa Menjadi Kasar

## Evidence focus

Chapter 19 treats boundaries as a leadership capability for defining what is acceptable, what is not, what conditions apply, and what action follows when the boundary is crossed. It distinguishes boundaries from hostility, punishment, and avoidance.

### 1. Boundaries are not simply interpersonal preferences

Workplace role expectations, rules, authority, and accountability create legitimate constraints. A leader may need to state a boundary because of safety, policy, capacity, ethics, role responsibility, or agreed standards.

### 2. Assertive communication differs from aggression and passivity

Assertiveness literature generally distinguishes expressing needs, rights, requests, or limits directly from aggressive communication that violates or disregards others' rights, and from passive communication that does not adequately represent one's position. The distinction is useful pedagogically but should not be treated as a universal psychological taxonomy.

### 3. Boundary effectiveness depends on clarity and follow-through

A boundary is difficult to act on when the other party cannot tell:
- what behavior or condition is at issue;
- what standard applies;
- what is expected instead;
- what happens if the condition is not met.

This supports a practical structure rather than relying on tone alone.

### 4. Psychological safety does not remove accountability

Frazier et al. (2017) found psychological safety associated with important work outcomes across a large meta-analytic sample. Chapter 16 already establishes that psychological safety should not be equated with absence of standards or consequences. Chapter 19 applies that distinction to boundary-setting.

### 5. Boundary-setting should not become humiliation

Research on abusive supervision and interpersonal mistreatment distinguishes accountability/discipline from hostile or degrading behavior. A leader can be firm while preserving dignity.

### 6. Not every boundary is negotiable

Some boundaries derive from law, safety requirements, ethics, organizational policy, or legitimate authority. Other boundaries are preferences or negotiation positions. The book should teach readers to distinguish these rather than presenting all boundaries as equally fixed.

## Editorial implications

- State the issue behaviorally and specifically.
- Name the standard or constraint where relevant.
- State the expected alternative.
- State the consequence/action proportionately.
- Avoid threats that cannot or will not be carried out.
- Do not use humiliation as enforcement.
- Do not call personal preference a rule.
- Do not call legitimate accountability “toxicity” merely because it is uncomfortable.
- Do not call aggression “firmness.”
- A boundary can include listening, explanation, accommodation, or negotiation when the underlying standard permits it.
- When the boundary concerns safety, law, fraud, harassment, or serious misconduct, formal procedures may supersede interpersonal negotiation.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Observe → Name → Standard → Limit → Consequence/Next Step → Follow Through → Review**

This is a book working model, not a validated boundary-setting taxonomy.
