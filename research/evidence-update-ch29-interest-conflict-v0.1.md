# Evidence Update — Chapter 29: Ketika Kepentingan Bertabrakan

## Evidence focus

Chapter 29 addresses legitimate and difficult conflicts of interest, priorities, resources, risks, and responsibilities. The chapter should teach negotiation and conflict reasoning without assuming every conflict has a win-win solution.

### 1. Conflict can arise from incompatible interests, not bad character

People can have legitimate but incompatible objectives because of role, accountability, resources, risk exposure, timing, or stakeholder obligations.

### 2. Positions and interests should be separated

A stated position is not always the underlying interest. Understanding interests can create additional options, but not every conflict disappears once interests are clarified.

### 3. Not all interests are equally legitimate

Some interests may conflict with law, policy, rights, safety, fiduciary duties, or organizational mandate. Negotiation does not require treating illegitimate demands as equivalent to legitimate constraints.

### 4. Resources and constraints create real trade-offs

Many organizational conflicts are allocation problems:
- budget;
- time;
- people;
- access;
- capacity;
- attention;
- risk tolerance.

A leader may need to make an explicit choice rather than manufacture consensus.

### 5. Negotiation involves alternatives and boundaries

A useful negotiation analysis considers:
- objective;
- interests;
- constraints;
- alternatives;
- minimum acceptable outcome where appropriate;
- possible trades;
- decision authority;
- consequences of no agreement.

These are practical tools, not universal formulas.

### 6. Integrative possibilities should be tested, not assumed

Some conflicts have compatible dimensions that permit trade-offs or package solutions. Others involve genuinely incompatible values, scarce resources, or rights. “Win-win” should therefore be treated as a possibility to investigate, not a moral requirement.

### 7. Process legitimacy matters

Even when an outcome is unpopular, transparent criteria, fair process, voice where appropriate, and clear decision rights can improve acceptance and reduce avoidable conflict.

### 8. Escalation can be appropriate

When negotiation reaches a boundary involving law, safety, authority, serious misconduct, or unresolved organizational mandate, escalation or formal decision mechanisms may be required.

## Editorial implications

- Distinguish interest conflict from interpersonal hostility.
- Avoid assuming bad motives.
- Make trade-offs visible.
- Test for compatible interests before declaring zero-sum conflict.
- Do not force consensus where legitimate disagreement remains.
- Protect non-negotiable standards and rights.
- Use explicit criteria for allocation decisions.
- Record who bears costs and risks.
- Separate negotiation from manipulation.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Issue → Actors → Positions → Interests → Constraints → Alternatives → Trade-offs → Agreement/Decision → Follow-through**

This is a book working model, not a validated negotiation taxonomy.
