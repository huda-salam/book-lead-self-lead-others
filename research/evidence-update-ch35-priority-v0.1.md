# Evidence Update — Chapter 35: Tidak Semua Hal Harus Dikerjakan

## Evidence focus

Chapter 35 treats prioritization as a management and leadership capability: choosing what deserves attention and resources while explicitly accepting what will be delayed, delegated, reduced, or stopped.

### Core evidence posture

- Human and organizational attention is limited.
- Multiple goals compete for time, resources, and cognitive capacity.
- Prioritization therefore requires choices and trade-offs rather than merely producing a longer task list.
- Goal selection and sequencing are affected by dependencies, deadlines, strategic importance, risk, and resource constraints.
- Urgency and importance are not identical.
- Activity volume is not equivalent to progress toward an outcome.
- Excessive priority switching can impose coordination and attention costs.
- Priorities should be revisited when assumptions, constraints, evidence, or organizational objectives change.

## Editorial implications

1. Priorities must be explicit enough that people know what is *not* priority.
2. A priority without resource allocation is often only a statement of preference.
3. Leaders should distinguish:
   - must do;
   - should do;
   - could do;
   - stop/not now.
4. A good priority decision names the objective and the trade-off.
5. “Busy” is not evidence of effectiveness.
6. Saying no is part of management, but should be grounded in objective, constraints, risk, authority, and timing.
7. Priority changes should have a reason and be communicated.
8. Emergency work should not silently displace everything else without making the trade-off visible.
9. Delegation is one possible response to capacity constraints, but its mechanics belong to Chapter 36.
10. Strategic choice is developed more deeply in Part VIII/Chapter 38.

## Proposed book synthesis

**Objective → Constraints → Consequences → Prioritize → Allocate → Communicate → Review**

This is a book working model, not a validated prioritization taxonomy.
