# Evidence Update — Chapter 16: Trust, Psychological Safety, Accountability, dan Respect

## Evidence focus

Bab ini menjelaskan kondisi relasional yang membuat orang dapat bekerja, berbicara, berbeda pendapat, meminta bantuan, mengakui kesalahan, dan tetap bertanggung jawab terhadap standar.

### 1. Psychological safety has a substantial research base
Frazier et al. (2017) meta-analyzed 136 independent samples involving more than 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups. Psychological safety was associated with multiple outcomes, with relationships varying by context and level of analysis. The evidence supports treating psychological safety as a meaningful organizational condition rather than as a synonym for comfort. citeturn0search0turn0search1

### 2. Psychological safety is distinct from trust
Edmondson's original team-level work conceptualized psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking. Later reviews and meta-analytic work distinguish psychological safety from related constructs such as trust, cohesion, and climate. The chapter should therefore avoid treating the terms as interchangeable. citeturn0search2turn0search3

### 3. Trust is multidimensional and relational
Organizational trust research commonly distinguishes trust in specific parties and dimensions such as ability, benevolence, and integrity. This supports treating trust as a judgment about whether another party is sufficiently reliable or trustworthy for a particular relationship/task, rather than as a general feeling of liking. citeturn0search4turn0search5

### 4. Accountability can coexist with psychological safety
Psychological safety research does not imply absence of standards, monitoring, correction, or consequences. In a healthy operating system, people can raise concerns and admit mistakes while still being accountable for commitments and conduct. This is a conceptual integration for the book, not a claim that one study validates a specific four-quadrant model.

### 5. Respect is behavioral and does not require agreement
Respect can be operationalized through listening, accurate representation, non-humiliating disagreement, fair treatment, and recognition of dignity. It does not require accepting every claim, tolerating abuse, or suspending standards. This extends Chapter 11 into the broader relational conditions chapter.

## Editorial implications

- Do not collapse trust, psychological safety, respect, and accountability into one “good culture” construct.
- Do not present psychological safety as “everyone feels comfortable.”
- Do not imply trust means unquestioning reliance.
- Preserve accountability for commitments, standards, rules, and harmful conduct.
- Distinguish interpersonal risk from substantive risk: a psychologically safe team can still face real operational, legal, financial, or safety risks.
- Avoid suggesting that psychological safety guarantees innovation, performance, or harmony.
- Preserve the possibility of disagreement, correction, escalation, and consequences.
- Treat trust as something that can be domain-specific and revisable.
- Avoid a simplistic “high safety/high accountability = always optimal” ranking.

## Proposed book synthesis

A relational operating model:

**Respect → Safety to Speak → Trust Calibration → Accountability → Repair/Learning**

This is a book synthesis, not a validated taxonomy or causal sequence.
